02 — The Psychology

Why do colors
feel different?

Color perception isn't just about the color itself. Your brain also responds to brightness, saturation, context, memories, culture and what is happening around you.

Red

Often associated with intensity, urgency, excitement and attention. It can feel powerful or stimulating.

Context matters — the same red can feel romantic, alarming, energetic or comforting.

Orange

Often connected with warmth, energy, playfulness and social environments.

Bright orange may feel energetic while a muted orange can feel much softer.

Yellow

Frequently associated with brightness, optimism, attention and mental stimulation.

Very bright yellow can also become visually intense.

Green

Commonly associated with nature, balance, freshness and restoration.

Your personal experiences with nature can influence how green feels to you.

Blue

Often linked with calmness, spaciousness, trust and coolness.

Different shades of blue can create very different impressions.

Purple

Can evoke creativity, mystery, imagination and unusual or luxurious associations.

Cultural associations with purple vary considerably.

Grey

Can feel neutral, quiet, serious, balanced or distant depending on its surroundings.

Grey is especially sensitive to the colors around it.

Dark Brown

Often connected with earthiness, stability, warmth, reliability and natural materials.

Texture and context can strongly affect how brown is perceived.

Black

Can communicate contrast, seriousness, sophistication, mystery or visual weight.

Its meaning changes dramatically depending on context.
03 — Myth vs Reality

Your favorite color
isn't your personality.

Color psychology is much more complicated — and way more interesting — than assigning one personality to one color.

Myth

“You like blue, so you're calm.”

Not necessarily. You might like blue because it reminds you of the ocean, your bedroom, a memory, or simply because you like how it looks.

Reality

Color associations are personal.

Psychology can identify patterns in how people respond to colors, but those patterns aren't rules that predict who someone is.
